🧱 Building block · Power

Voltage: DC vs AC

Voltage is the push that makes electrons move. It comes in two flavours.

  • DC (Direct Current) flows one way, steady. Batteries, USB, phone chargers and every Arduino pin use DC (usually 3.3 V or 5 V).
  • AC (Alternating Current) keeps reversing. The wall socket in India gives about 230 V at 50 Hz, which means 50 full back-and-forth cycles every second.
  • AC travels long distances easily, but electronics need DC. That is why every charger has an adapter that converts AC to DC.
⚠ Never connect a wall socket to a breadboard
🧱 Building block

Resistor: the traffic cop

A resistor limits how much current flows. Think of a road that suddenly gets narrow: fewer cars get through.

  • Ohm's law: V = I × R. With the same voltage, more resistance means less current.
  • Measured in ohms (Ω). The coloured bands on its body are a code for the value.
  • Used to protect LEDs, to build voltage dividers, and as pull-up or pull-down resistors for buttons.

Capacitor: tiny charge bucket

Two metal plates with a thin insulator in between. Connect a battery and charge piles up on the plates. Remove it, and the capacitor can release that charge in a flash.

  • Measured in farads. Hobby parts are µF (millionths of a farad).
  • Smooths bumpy power, filters noise, times things (RC circuits), and powers camera flashes.
  • The ceiling fan at home has one. It gives the motor its starting push.

👀 Sensor · Input

Potentiometer: the knob

A resistor you can turn. It has 3 pins: one end to 5 V, the other end to GND, and a middle wiper that slides along the track.

  • Turn the knob and the wiper splits the track into two parts, giving any voltage between 0 and 5 V. This is a voltage divider.
  • Volume knobs, light dimmers and joysticks all use one.
  • Drag the knob on the right. Watch the wiper and the bulb.

🧠 The Brain

Microcontroller: a computer on a chip

It has a CPU (thinks), memory (remembers your program) and pins (talk to the outside world). It repeats the same 3 steps forever:

  • 1 · READ an input pin (is the button pressed?)
  • 2 · DECIDE using your code (if pressed then...)
  • 3 · ACT by changing an output pin (turn the LED on)

The microwave, washing machine, TV remote and AC at home all hide one inside.

void loop() {
  int s = digitalRead(BUTTON);       // 1 READ
  if (s == HIGH) {                       // 2 DECIDE
    digitalWrite(LED, HIGH);            // 3 ACT
  } else digitalWrite(LED, LOW);
}

💪 Actuator · Output

LED: a one-way light

An LED is a diode, a one-way valve for current, that glows when current passes through it.

  • Anode (+) is the long leg. Cathode (−) is the short leg, next to the flat edge. Connect them backwards and nothing flows.
  • An LED has almost no resistance of its own. Without a series resistor it draws too much current and burns out.
  • Try both buttons on the right.
Typical: 2 V drop, 10–20 mAR = (Vsupply − Vled) / I
💪 Actuator · Output

Motors: electricity into motion

A wire carrying current inside a magnetic field gets pushed sideways. Bend the wire into a loop and the two sides get pushed in opposite directions, so the loop spins.

  • Force = field × current × length. More current means more push.
  • A commutator flips the current every half turn so the push always keeps the same spin direction.
  • Three kinds you will use: DC motor (spins freely), servo (goes to an exact angle), stepper (moves in small precise steps).

👀 Input 1 of 6

Push button: the simplest sensor

Press it and two pieces of metal touch, which closes the circuit. The pin sees 5 V (HIGH). Let go and it sees 0 V (LOW).

  • A pull-down resistor (10 kΩ) ties the pin to GND when the button is open. Without it the pin floats and reads random noise.
  • Real buttons bounce. The contacts chatter for a few milliseconds, so code often adds a short delay (debounce).
pinMode(2, INPUT);
if (digitalRead(2) == HIGH) { /* pressed! */ }

👀 Input 2 of 6

Potentiometer as analog input

Buttons are on or off. A pot gives a smooth range. The microcontroller's ADC (analog-to-digital converter) turns that voltage into a number.

  • Arduino Uno: 10-bit ADC, so 0 to 1023 (each step is about 4.9 mV).
  • ESP32: 12-bit ADC, so 0 to 4095.
  • map() rescales the number, for example 0–1023 to a servo angle of 0–180°.
int v = analogRead(A0);
int angle = map(v, 0, 1023, 0, 180);

👀 Input 3 of 6

Photoresistor: a light-sensitive resistor

An LDR (light dependent resistor) changes its resistance with light. Bright light drops it to a few kΩ. Darkness raises it to hundreds of kΩ.

  • Put it in a voltage divider with a fixed 10 kΩ resistor and the pin sees a voltage that follows the light.
  • The Arduino compares that number to a threshold. Below it means dark, so switch the lamp on.
  • This is how automatic street lights, night lamps and screen auto-brightness work.

👀 Input 4 of 6

Ultrasonic sensor: a bat's sonar

The HC-SR04 shouts a 40 kHz chirp you cannot hear, then listens for the echo. Longer echo means farther object.

  • TRIG pin: a 10 µs pulse starts the chirp. ECHO pin: stays HIGH until the echo returns.
  • distance = time × 0.0343 cm/µs ÷ 2 (divide by 2 because the sound goes there and back).
  • Range is about 2 cm to 4 m. Used in parking sensors, water-level meters and obstacle-avoiding robots.

👀 Input 5 of 6

Soil moisture: does the plant need water?

Two prongs go into the soil. Wet soil conducts electricity and changes capacitance differently from dry soil, so the sensor outputs an analog number.

  • Cheap resistive probes corrode in weeks. Capacitive ones last far longer.
  • Calibrate it: note the reading in dry air and in a glass of water, and use those as your limits.
  • In this demo, a higher number means drier (like many capacitive modules).

👀 Input 6 of 6

IR sensor: invisible light radar

An infrared LED sends out invisible light. A photodiode next to it watches for a reflection. If something bounces the light back, the output flips.

  • Most modules output LOW when an object is detected, and an onboard LED lights up.
  • Light-coloured surfaces reflect IR. Black surfaces absorb it, which is how line-following robots see the track.
  • Sunlight also contains IR, so it can confuse the sensor outdoors.
🧠 Brain 1

Arduino Uno: the friendly starter

A microcontroller (ATmega328P) on a board with USB, power and labelled pin headers. Write C/C++ in the Arduino IDE, press upload, done.

  • Great for learning: huge community, thousands of tutorials and libraries.
  • No built-in Wi-Fi or Bluetooth. Add a module, or pick an ESP32.

More brains to explore

Hover (or tap) a card to flip it. A microcontroller runs one program forever. A microprocessor board like the Raspberry Pi runs a full operating system.

Raspberry Pi Pico

RP2040 microcontroller
Dual-core 133 MHz. Programs in MicroPython or C. Cheap and powerful.

Best for: learning Python on hardware.

Raspberry Pi

Tiny Linux computer
A full computer with HDMI, USB, Wi-Fi and a camera port. Runs Linux.

Best for: vision, AI, servers, media centres.

micro:bit

Built for beginners
25 LEDs, 2 buttons, motion sensor and radio on board. Block coding or Python.

Best for: first-time makers.

Arduino Nano

Uno, but tiny
Same ATmega328P brain as the Uno, in a breadboard-friendly size.

Best for: compact permanent projects.

STM32

"Blue Pill" ARM chip
32-bit ARM Cortex-M chips used in real products: drones, cars, appliances.

Best for: serious embedded work.

NodeMCU / ESP8266

Cheap Wi-Fi board
The older sibling of the ESP32: Wi-Fi only, very cheap, great for simple IoT.

Best for: budget smart-home gadgets.
💪 Actuator 1 of 2

Servo motor: goes exactly where you say

Inside: a small motor, gears, a position-sensing potentiometer and a control circuit. You send a pulse, and it turns to that angle and holds it.

  • A pulse is sent every 20 ms. Its width sets the angle: 1 ms = 0°, 1.5 ms = 90°, 2 ms = 180°.
  • 3 wires: brown = GND, red = 5 V, orange = signal.
  • Used in robot arms, door latches, camera gimbals and RC steering.
servo.attach(9);
servo.write(90);   // go to 90°

💪 Actuator 2 of 2

DC motor: spins, spins, spins

Apply voltage and it spins. Speed follows the average voltage. Reverse the polarity and it spins the other way.

  • PWM (analogWrite) switches power on and off very fast. A longer ON time means a higher average voltage and a faster motor.
  • Direction: an H-bridge driver (L298N, L293D) swaps the polarity for you.
  • ⚠ A pin can supply only ~20 mA and a motor wants far more, so never drive a motor straight from a pin. Use a driver or transistor, plus a flyback diode.
